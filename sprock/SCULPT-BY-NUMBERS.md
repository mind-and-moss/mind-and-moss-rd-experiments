# MONSTER8 — SCULPT BY NUMBERS

The armature is `structure_outcrop.py`. This is the map that goes over it.

**Division of labour:** the machine gives you massing and a zone map. You bring
detail, colour and magic. Nothing below tells you how it should *look* — each
number tells you what that surface *is*, and what physically happened to it.
Sculpt the cause; the look follows.

---

## The object

**420 × 250 × 190 mm.** The shelf product is ~300 mm long, so this is a little
over half again as big in every direction — roughly 2.5× the volume.

Flat-lying limestone outcrop, terraced by bedding planes, dissolution cave at
the front-left, flat perch on top. **Every step in the reference photo is a
bed edge** — which is why the bed stack drives the entire shape and why the
terraces are an output of hardness rather than something drawn on.

| Bed | Thickness | Hardness | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| `A1_platform` | 38.0 mm | 5 | well-cemented grainstone — the plinth |
| `A2_marl` | 20.9 mm | **1** | **the rotten one — this is the cave** |
| `A3_cap` | 39.9 mm | 5 | massive bed — roof of the cave, overhangs A2 |
| `A4_bench` | 30.4 mm | 3 | ordinary biomicrite — the wide mid terrace |
| `A5_parting` | 17.1 mm | 2 | thin marly step |
| `A6_perch` | 43.7 mm | 4 | crest bed — the flat top |

Retreat is set only by hardness: h5 → 0 mm, h4 → 5, h3 → 11, h2 → 19,
**h1 → 38 mm**. The terrace profile falls out of that. Nothing was styled.

---

## The story, in one paragraph

Shallow warm sea, six beds laid down. One of them — `A2` — came in muddy and
clay-rich while the rest came in clean. Uplift, then fresh water working along
the joints. The clay-rich bed dissolved and crumbled far faster than the ones
above and below it, so it retreated into the hill. `A3` above lost its support
and became an overhang. **The cave is that retreat.** Nobody dug it. What
you're sculpting is a bed that left.

## THE NUMBERS

### 1 — HARD BED FACE (red)
Vertical faces of `A1`, `A3`, `A6`. Well-cemented rock that stood its ground.
- Stands **proud** of everything around it
- Edges **rounded and worn** — these took the weather longest
- Surface relatively **smooth**; hard rock does not crumble, it polishes
- *Least* detail on the whole piece. Resist filling it.

### 2 — SOFT BAND (orange)
Vertical faces of `A2` and `A5`. Clay-rich rock that gave up.
- Set **back 19–38 mm** from the hard beds — the single most important read
- **Horizontal scoring**, dense and irregular, following the bedding
- **Crumbly, granular, fractured**. This is the only zone that gets busy.
- Undercut it slightly where it meets the bed above

### 3 — LEDGE TOP (blue)
Upward faces of every terrace. Exposed bedding planes.
- **Flat, very slightly dished** — water sits and pools here
- Shallow **solution pits and runnels** draining toward the front edge
- Front lip **rounded over**; back of the ledge stays crisper
- **Fossils go here**, along the bedding, flush and never proud

### 4 — MOUTH RIM (pink)
The lip of the cave, front-left.
- **Flared and smooth** — water moved through here for a very long time
- Fully **rounded over**, no sharp edge anywhere a fish can meet
- **Widest at the outside**, narrowing inward. Never a wedge.

### 5 — CAVE INSIDE (near-black)
Interior surfaces.
- **Minimal detail.** Nobody sees it and detail here is wasted labour
- Smooth, no sharp fragments
- Keep it **dark** — it is the darkest thing in the piece, and the shadow-first
  gate is what makes the cave read from across a room

### 6 — UNDERCUT (purple)
Downward-facing rock over a retreated soft band.
- **Deep horizontal cut**, the shadow line of the whole piece
- Crisp at the top where hard rock broke; ragged at the bottom where soft rock
  crumbled away
- Push this deeper than feels sensible. It is what sells the overhang.

### 7 — MEDIUM FACE (green)
Vertical faces of `A4`. Ordinary rock, ordinary weathering.
- Between 1 and 2 in every respect
- Some horizontal grain, moderate relief
- The **quiet zone** — it exists so the others can be loud

### 8 — PERCH TOP (yellow)
The flat top of `A6`. Longest-exposed surface on the piece.
- **Most weathered surface here.** Pitted, rounded, softened
- Solution hollows, a shallow basin that holds water
- Every edge rounded — nothing on top has been sheltered from anything

### 9 — BURIED EDGE (brown)
Bottom 14 mm, everywhere.
- **Feather it into the substrate.** No hard visible waterline
- Undercuts and pockets so gravel banks against it and it stays dig-stable
- Detail here is cheap: most of it disappears under sand

## Order of work

1. Cut every **6 (undercut)** first — deepest cuts, while there's most material
2. Then **2 (soft bands)** — score and set back
3. Then **3 and 8 (tops)** — dish, pit, drain
4. Then **1 and 7 (faces)** — round and grain
5. **4 (mouth rim)** near the end, so it stays smooth
6. **9 (buried edge)** last, fitted to the actual tank
7. **5 (cave inside)** — barely touch it

**Wear-geometry rule that governs all of it:** detail survives in recesses and
is destroyed on exposed faces. If a surface faces the sky or the room, it
should be *smoother* than a surface tucked under a ledge. Getting this
backwards is what makes fake rock look fake.

# UPDATE — zones for the MATURE structure

The map above describes the six-slab armature. The story-conformant build adds
four systems, and each needs its own treatment. Same principle throughout:
**the number tells you what the surface is and what happened to it.**

### 10 — GRIKE WALL
The two facing walls of an open joint, running down through the stack.
- **Near-vertical and near-parallel** — a joint is a fracture, so the two sides
  once fitted together and still nearly do
- **Fluted vertically** by water running down, not horizontally
- Rounded and widened at the top where it has been open longest; **tighter and
  sharper toward the bottom** where water reached later
- The two walls should look like they'd close if you pushed them together.
  That's the tell that it's a joint and not a carved slot.

### 11 — EMBAYMENT SCAR
The broken face where the cap collapsed over the cave (Episode 5).
- **Fresher-looking than everything around it** — this is the youngest surface
  on the piece
- Angular, joint-bounded, stepped — it failed along fractures that were
  already there, so its edges are straight, not curved
- Less rounding than any other exposed face. It hasn't had time.

### 12 — TALUS BLOCK
The fallen rock at the foot.
- **Rectangular, not rounded** — these are joint-bounded blocks that broke off,
  not river cobbles
- Edges knocked off from the fall and from decades of being shifted
- **Bed lines run through them at random angles** — they landed how they landed,
  so their bedding does not line up with the outcrop's. This one detail sells
  the whole story.
- Feather the bases into substrate; some should be more buried than others

### 13 — SOLUTION BASIN
Shallow dish on a ledge top where standing water sinks in at a joint crossing.
- **Smooth, rounded, dished** — dissolution, not impact
- Deepest at the centre, feathering out at the rim, with **no hard edge**
- A drainage runnel leading out of it toward the ledge's low side
- Mineral staining ring at the old waterline, if you want one place for colour
  to concentrate

## What the story tells you that the geometry can't

Read `THE-STORY.md` alongside this. Two clauses matter most for sculpting:

**`A2` is the bed that should never have been there.** A river switched course
and dumped clay onto a clean shelf. Everything in this piece — the cave, the
overhang, the collapse, the talus — descends from that one accident. When you
are deciding how rotten to make the marl band, the answer is: rotten enough
that it explains all of it.

**The piece is a fragment** (Episode 7). Its flat faces are fresh fracture
surfaces, not sawn ones. That is why they sit against glass without looking
manufactured — and it means the flat faces should carry a *little* of the
angularity of a broken joint face, not be sanded dead flat.
